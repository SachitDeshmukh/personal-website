Understanding Language by Learning Logic. Maybe?

Exploring how the scientific study of language and reasoning converge.

05 NOVEMBER 2020 • SACHIT DESHMUKH • 10 MIN READ

Verbal communication can be complicated!

Language, the most fundamental element of communication, is a fairly versatile entity. The placement of words, the context in which the words are used, and the tone of the speaker influences what meaning the speaker wants to convey.

Take for instance the sentence:

Buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo buffalo buffalo Buffalo buffalo.

Now this sentence, if looked at a glance, makes no sense. But trust me when I say, that is a sensible sentence. To understand it, we need to look at the meaning of the word - buffalo. It would be safe to assume that this word has more than 1 meaning. Otherwise, I just repeated the same word eight times and would be ridiculed if I had still claimed that it has meaning.

Firstly, Buffalo - with a capital initial - refers to the city of Buffalo in New York. Secondly, buffalo is a noun that refers to the American bison. And lastly, buffalo is a verb, which means “to confuse”.[1]

After applying these meanings, the sentence translates to:

“The bison from the city of Buffalo, that the bison from the city of Buffalo confuse, in turn confuse those other bison from the city of Buffalo.”

Long story short, language is complicated. And given its daily usage, it is very important to make sure that what we intend to convey gets conveyed to the other person.

Symbolic representations in logic simplify language

I am pretty sure most of us here know more than one language - English and our mother tongue. Some of us even go so far as to study an additional language: Sanskrit, German, French. We learn many such languages, but what we don't learn is the very essence of any language.

What is language? Why are words formed? How do I know that this is the sound of a word? When does this word mean this? Where can this word make sense? All these questions are answered by a field of science called Linguistics.

In one line, linguistics is the scientific study of human language. Human language comprises everyday statements, conversations between people, political debates, and everything which can be called a set of words or symbols. As symbolic logic provides framework to prove arguments valid or invalid, and derive valid inferences from existing arguments, I want to know how does learning symbolic logic help the study of linguistics.

Consider the following sentences.
  1. I like dogs, but I hate cats.
  2. I did my part of the assignment whereas she did not.
  3. It was very cloudy today, yet not a single drop of rain was seen.

Although the words “but”, “yet”, and “whereas” are different, each one is used to convey the same meaning - that there is something in contrast to the previous statement.

English being a rich language, such occurrences of synonymous words is not uncommon. Its Germanic roots, along with the influence of Romantic languages - French and Latin, have resulted in multiple words that essentially mean the same thing.[2]

As a part of symbolic logic, we come across many such sets of words, which seemingly are unique, but logically are the same. With reference to arguments, words which are lexically unique, but mean the same would be symbolized with the same constant or operator through the argument.

In technical terms, symbolic logic studies formal languages to understand certain aspects of natural languages.[3] Whatever we learn is a representation of the structure of natural languages.

Is all of language simple enough to break into logical frameworks?

In predicate logic, we deal with the assertion or denial of qualities, of one or many individuals individually, or in comparison to others.

  • “Disney movies are musicals”
  • “Non-binary pronouns are more inclusive than gendered pronouns”

But language is not all about assertions. In fact, these assertions are the result of some kind of research.[4]

Language, as we use, goes beyond definite claims, and talks about possible and necessary scenarios too. These propositions of necessity and possibility are dealt in the field of modal logic.

Having spoken English for about the entirety of my life, I know the difference between “will” and “would” when referring to future situations. But this difference is better explained with the concepts of modal logic. When we introduce the aspect of frame of reference in time, the following statements are logically unique propositions.

  • I guessed that Mumbai Indians will win, and
  • I guessed that Mumbai Indians would win.

The former can be determined true or false only after it has been uttered, whereas the later has been uttered some time before this utterance, and hence its truth value was determinable then, not after now.[3]

What I am trying to say is that logic formalizes and interprets a language. It helps in the understanding of this semantic study of language - study of meaning in human language. That logic and language cannot be learnt without each other is as true as is the spherical nature of earth.

Study of logic supporting language might be anecdotal

In spite of that, a research conducted by college professors in Israel regarding the influence of learning logic programming on understanding of argumentation texts found out that taking LP classes did not significantly increase the understanding of arguments in students.[5]

67% of the 11th graders who participated self-reported disagreement regarding the aforementioned hypothesized influence.

The takeaway here is that this field of logic and linguistics is niche, and not all will have to formally learn the benefits of logic in studying linguistics. Those who study language, might find new ways to explain why some things are the way they are.

Language is a developing field. The number of total languages present keeps fluctuating.[6] Every 100 minutes, the English language welcomes a new word to its ambiguously vast vocabulary. [7] In an effort to understand these developments, logic too, never stops improving. And its quest to formulate a universal structure to all the natural languages continues. Our role as speakers of language is to constantly provide logic its subject matter and facilitate its growth.

And only then, the following sentence can be proved logically sound and linguistically accurate.[8]

"Colorless green ideas sleep furiously..."

— Noam Chomsky
